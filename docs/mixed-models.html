17 Mixed models and variance components

17.1 Fixed and random effects

A factor in a model is treated as

  • fixed when its levels are chosen deliberately and conclusions apply only to those levels: fertiliser doses, irrigation schedules, a set of released varieties being compared;
  • random when its levels are a random sample from a larger population, and interest lies in the variability of that population: blocks, years, locations, animals, or genotypes sampled from a breeding population.

A model containing both is a mixed model. For a random factor we estimate a variance component, not the effect of each level. Variance components are central in plant breeding (genetic variance, heritability), in sampling (variation between and within plots) and in multi-location trials (genotype × environment variance).

Mixed models also handle designs with several error terms (split plots, repeated measures) and unbalanced data in a unified way.

In R, mixed models are fitted with lmer() from the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). The lmerTest package adds F tests with Satterthwaite or Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom. Random effects are written as (1 | factor): “a random intercept for each level of factor”.

17.2 Estimating variance components by the ANOVA method

For a one-way random model \(y_{ij} = \mu + g_i + e_{ij}\) with \(g_i \sim N(0, \sigma_g^2)\) and \(e_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma_e^2)\), the expected mean squares are

Source df MS E(MS)
Genotypes \(g - 1\) \(M_g\) \(\sigma_e^2 + r\sigma_g^2\)
Error \((g-1)(r-1)\) \(M_e\) \(\sigma_e^2\)

(the same holds in an RCBD, with blocks removed). Equating mean squares to their expectations gives

\[\hat\sigma_e^2 = M_e, \qquad \hat\sigma_g^2 = \frac{M_g - M_e}{r}\]

17.2.1 Example: genetic parameters in a potato trial

We return to the 28 potato genotypes in an RCBD with three replications at La Molina (plrv, Chapter 14). If these genotypes are regarded as a sample from a breeding population, the genotypic variance and heritability describe how much of the variation in yield is genetic.

library(agricolae)
data(plrv)
plrv$Rep <- factor(plrv$Rep)
lm02 <- droplevels(subset(plrv, Locality == "LM-02"))

tab <- summary(aov(Yield ~ Rep + Genotype, data = lm02))[[1]]
tab
#>             Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value      Pr(>F)    
#> Rep          2    480   240.1    8.97     0.00043 ***
#> Genotype    27   3890   144.1    5.38 0.000000077 ***
#> Residuals   54   1445    26.8                        
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
r  <- 3
Mg <- tab[2, "Mean Sq"]
Me <- tab[3, "Mean Sq"]
sigma2_g <- (Mg - Me) / r          # genotypic variance
sigma2_e <- Me                     # error (environmental) variance
sigma2_p <- sigma2_g + sigma2_e    # phenotypic variance
xbar <- mean(lm02$Yield)

c(sigma2_g = sigma2_g, sigma2_e = sigma2_e, sigma2_p = sigma2_p,
  H2_plot  = sigma2_g / sigma2_p,                    # broad-sense heritability, plot basis
  H2_mean  = sigma2_g / (sigma2_g + sigma2_e / r),   # heritability, entry-mean basis
  GCV = sqrt(sigma2_g) / xbar * 100,                 # genotypic coefficient of variation
  PCV = sqrt(sigma2_p) / xbar * 100)                 # phenotypic coefficient of variation
#> sigma2_g sigma2_e sigma2_p  H2_plot  H2_mean      GCV      PCV 
#>  39.1015  26.7559  65.8574   0.5937   0.8143  18.0493  23.4243

Broad-sense heritability on a plot basis is 0.59, and on an entry-mean basis (relevant when selection is based on genotype means over three replications) it is 0.81. The genotypic coefficient of variation (18%) is a large part of the phenotypic coefficient of variation (23.4%), indicating considerable genetic variability for yield.

17.3 Estimating variance components by REML

The ANOVA method is simple but can give negative estimates and does not extend easily to unbalanced data. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the default in lmer(), avoids these problems.

library(lme4)
library(lmerTest)
m1 <- lmer(Yield ~ Rep + (1 | Genotype), data = lm02)
summary(m1)
#> Linear mixed model fit by REML. t-tests use Satterthwaite's method [
#> lmerModLmerTest]
#> Formula: Yield ~ Rep + (1 | Genotype)
#>    Data: lm02
#> 
#> REML criterion at convergence: 551.5
#> 
#> Scaled residuals: 
#>     Min      1Q  Median      3Q     Max 
#> -2.0191 -0.5171  0.0902  0.4563  1.9387 
#> 
#> Random effects:
#>  Groups   Name        Variance Std.Dev.
#>  Genotype (Intercept) 39.1     6.25    
#>  Residual             26.8     5.17    
#> Number of obs: 84, groups:  Genotype, 28
#> 
#> Fixed effects:
#>             Estimate Std. Error    df t value Pr(>|t|)    
#> (Intercept)    37.15       1.53 47.51   24.23  < 2e-16 ***
#> Rep2           -1.80       1.38 54.00   -1.30  0.19771    
#> Rep3           -5.73       1.38 54.00   -4.14  0.00012 ***
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
#> 
#> Correlation of Fixed Effects:
#>      (Intr) Rep2  
#> Rep2 -0.451       
#> Rep3 -0.451  0.500
vc <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(m1))
vc[, c("grp", "vcov", "sdcor")]
#>        grp  vcov sdcor
#> 1 Genotype 39.10 6.253
#> 2 Residual 26.76 5.173

With balanced data, REML reproduces the ANOVA estimates (\(\hat\sigma_g^2\) = 39.1, \(\hat\sigma_e^2\) = 26.76). The significance of the genotypic variance is tested by a likelihood-ratio test that compares the model with and without the random effect:

ranova(m1)
#> ANOVA-like table for random-effects: Single term deletions
#> 
#> Model:
#> Yield ~ Rep + (1 | Genotype)
#>                npar logLik AIC  LRT Df Pr(>Chisq)    
#> <none>            5   -276 562                       
#> (1 | Genotype)    4   -290 587 27.5  1 0.00000016 ***
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

The genotypic variance is highly significant.

The likelihood-ratio test for a variance component tests a parameter on the boundary of its space (\(\sigma_g^2 = 0\)), so the reported p-value is conservative; halving it gives a closer approximation.

17.4 Multi-environment trials

The full plrv data include the 28 genotypes in six environments. A mixed model separates the genotypic variance from the genotype × environment (G × E) interaction variance. Environments are treated as fixed here, and replications are nested within environments:

m2 <- lmer(Yield ~ Locality + (1 | Locality:Rep) + (1 | Genotype) +
             (1 | Genotype:Locality), data = plrv)
vc2 <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(m2))
vc2[, c("grp", "vcov")]
#>                 grp   vcov
#> 1 Genotype:Locality 46.327
#> 2          Genotype 26.298
#> 3      Locality:Rep  2.076
#> 4          Residual 37.031
ranova(m2)
#> ANOVA-like table for random-effects: Single term deletions
#> 
#> Model:
#> Yield ~ Locality + (1 | Locality:Rep) + (1 | Genotype) + (1 | Genotype:Locality)
#>                         npar logLik  AIC   LRT Df Pr(>Chisq)    
#> <none>                    10  -1769 3558                        
#> (1 | Locality:Rep)         9  -1772 3563   7.0  1     0.0082 ** 
#> (1 | Genotype)             9  -1781 3580  24.7  1 0.00000065 ***
#> (1 | Genotype:Locality)    9  -1834 3687 131.0  1    < 2e-16 ***
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

All variance components are significant. The G × E variance (46.3) is larger than the genotypic variance (26.3): the genotypes rank differently in different environments, as the interaction plot in Chapter 9 suggested.

Heritability on an entry-mean basis across \(e\) environments and \(r\) replications is

\[H^2 = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_{ge}^2/e + \sigma_e^2/(re)}\]

s2g  <- vc2$vcov[vc2$grp == "Genotype"]
s2ge <- vc2$vcov[vc2$grp == "Genotype:Locality"]
s2e  <- vc2$vcov[vc2$grp == "Residual"]
e <- 6; r <- 3
s2g / (s2g + s2ge / e + s2e / (r * e))
#> [1] 0.729

17.4.1 BLUPs: predicting genotype values

When genotypes are random, their predicted effects are best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs). BLUPs shrink the observed genotype means towards the overall mean, more so when the heritability is low, and they are widely used to rank genotypes in breeding programmes.

blup <- ranef(m2)$Genotype
blup$Genotype <- rownames(blup)
names(blup)[1] <- "BLUP"
head(blup[order(-blup$BLUP), ], 6)
#>         BLUP Genotype
#> 141.28 6.624   141.28
#> Unica  6.148    Unica
#> 319.20 5.896   319.20
#> 235.6  5.806    235.6
#> 157.26 4.579   157.26
#> 450.3  4.029    450.3

The genotype Unica (a released variety) has the highest predicted genetic value for yield across environments.

17.5 Split-plot designs as mixed models

A split-plot design has two error terms because main plots and sub-plots are units of different sizes. In a mixed model, both are represented by random effects: blocks and main plots (variety within block) are random. For the oats experiment (Chapter 14):

library(MASS)
m3 <- lmer(Y ~ V * N + (1 | B / V), data = oats)
anova(m3)                               # Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
#> Type III Analysis of Variance Table with Satterthwaite's method
#>     Sum Sq Mean Sq NumDF DenDF F value  Pr(>F)    
#> V      526     263     2    10    1.49    0.27    
#> N    20021    6674     3    45   37.69 2.5e-12 ***
#> V:N    322      54     6    45    0.30    0.93    
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
anova(m3, ddf = "Kenward-Roger")        # Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom
#> Type III Analysis of Variance Table with Kenward-Roger's method
#>     Sum Sq Mean Sq NumDF DenDF F value  Pr(>F)    
#> V      526     263     2    10    1.49    0.27    
#> N    20021    6674     3    45   37.69 2.5e-12 ***
#> V:N    322      54     6    45    0.30    0.93    
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

The F tests and their denominator degrees of freedom (10 for varieties, 45 for nitrogen and the interaction) are exactly those of the classical split-plot ANOVA. The variance components are

VarCorr(m3)
#>  Groups   Name        Std.Dev.
#>  V:B      (Intercept) 10.3    
#>  B        (Intercept) 14.6    
#>  Residual             13.3

The mixed-model approach has two advantages: it works with missing data, and emmeans computes correct standard errors for any comparison, combining the error terms as needed.

library(emmeans)
emm_V <- emmeans(m3, ~ V)
pairs(emm_V)            # main-plot comparisons use the larger main-plot error
#>  contrast                 estimate   SE df t.ratio p.value
#>  Golden.rain - Marvellous    -5.29 7.08 10  -0.748  0.7419
#>  Golden.rain - Victory        6.88 7.08 10   0.971  0.6104
#>  Marvellous - Victory        12.17 7.08 10   1.719  0.2458
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: N 
#> Degrees-of-freedom method: kenward-roger 
#> P value adjustment: tukey method for comparing a family of 3 estimates
emm_N <- emmeans(m3, ~ N)
emm_N
#>  N      emmean   SE   df lower.CL upper.CL
#>  0.0cwt   79.4 7.17 6.79     62.3     96.5
#>  0.2cwt   98.9 7.17 6.79     81.8    116.0
#>  0.4cwt  114.2 7.17 6.79     97.2    131.3
#>  0.6cwt  123.4 7.17 6.79    106.3    140.5
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: V 
#> Degrees-of-freedom method: kenward-roger 
#> Confidence level used: 0.95

17.6 Testing the significance of contrasts in mixed models

Contrasts (Chapter 15) are tested in mixed models with emmeans::contrast(), which uses the appropriate standard error and degrees of freedom. For the four equally spaced nitrogen levels, orthogonal polynomial contrasts are obtained with method = "poly":

contrast(emm_N, method = "poly")
#>  contrast  estimate    SE df t.ratio p.value
#>  linear       147.3 14.03 45  10.503  <.0001
#>  quadratic    -10.3  6.27 45  -1.647  0.1065
#>  cubic         -2.0 14.03 45  -0.143  0.8873
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: V 
#> Degrees-of-freedom method: kenward-roger

The linear trend is highly significant, while the quadratic and cubic components are not, confirming the conclusion of Chapter 15. Custom contrasts are specified as a list of coefficients, for example nitrogen applied (any level) versus no nitrogen:

contrast(emm_N, list("N applied vs none" = c(-3, 1, 1, 1) / 3))
#>  contrast          estimate   SE df t.ratio p.value
#>  N applied vs none     32.8 3.62 45   9.050  <.0001
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: V 
#> Degrees-of-freedom method: kenward-roger

Applying nitrogen increased yield by 32.8 units on average over the three levels.
