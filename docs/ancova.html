16 Analysis of covariance

Sometimes part of the variation among experimental units can be measured but not controlled by blocking: the stand count of each plot, the initial weight of each animal, the yield of each tree before treatments were applied. Such a variable, measured on each unit and related to the response but not affected by the treatments, is called a covariate (concomitant variable).

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) combines ANOVA with regression. It

  1. removes from the error the variation explained by the covariate, which increases precision, and
  2. adjusts the treatment means to a common value of the covariate, which removes bias due to chance differences in the covariate between treatments.

16.1 Model

For an RCBD with one covariate \(x\):

\[y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \gamma\,(x_{ij} - \bar x_{..}) + e_{ij}\]

where \(\gamma\) is the common regression coefficient of \(y\) on \(x\) within treatments and blocks. The adjusted treatment mean is

\[\bar y_{i(\text{adj})} = \bar y_{i.} - \hat\gamma\,(\bar x_{i.} - \bar x_{..})\]

16.1.1 Assumptions

In addition to the ANOVA assumptions:

  1. the covariate is not affected by the treatments (measure it before treatments are applied, or choose a variable the treatments cannot influence);
  2. the relationship between \(y\) and \(x\) is linear;
  3. the regression slope is the same for all treatments (homogeneity of regression slopes).

16.2 Example: apple trees

Pearce (1953) reported an experiment on apple trees at East Malling with six soil-management treatments (A–E, and S, the standard practice of keeping the land clean in summer) in four blocks. The response is the crop yield after treatment, and the covariate is the yield of the same tree (number of boxes of fruit) in the four seasons before the treatments were applied. The data are pearce.apple in agridat.

library(agridat)
apple <- pearce.apple
head(apple)
#>   block trt prev yield
#> 1    B1   A  8.2   287
#> 2    B1   B  8.2   271
#> 3    B1   C  6.8   234
#> 4    B1   D  5.7   189
#> 5    B1   E  6.1   210
#> 6    B1   S  7.6   222

Since the covariate was measured before the treatments, it cannot have been affected by them. A quick check confirms that it does not differ among treatments:

summary(aov(prev ~ block + trt, data = apple))
#>             Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F)  
#> block        3  25.63    8.54    5.29  0.011 *
#> trt          5   7.39    1.48    0.92  0.497  
#> Residuals   15  24.23    1.62                 
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
plot(yield ~ prev, data = apple, pch = as.character(apple$trt),
     col = as.integer(apple$block), cex = 1.1,
     xlab = "Previous yield (covariate)", ylab = "Yield after treatment")
legend("topleft", legend = levels(apple$block), col = 1:4, pch = 15,
       title = "Block", bty = "n")

The plot shows a strong linear relationship between previous and current yield: trees that cropped well before the treatments also cropped well afterwards.

16.2.1 ANOVA without the covariate 

summary(aov(yield ~ block + trt, data = apple))
#>             Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value  Pr(>F)    
#> block        3  47853   15951    10.2 0.00065 ***
#> trt          5    750     150     0.1 0.99146    
#> Residuals   15  23432    1562                    
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Ignoring the covariate, the treatments appear to have no effect at all (\(F = 0.10\), \(p = 0.99\)), and the error mean square is 1562.

16.2.2 ANCOVA

Add the covariate to the model before the treatment term, so that treatments are tested after adjustment for the covariate:

ancova <- aov(yield ~ block + prev + trt, data = apple)
summary(ancova)
#>             Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value      Pr(>F)    
#> block        3  47853   15951   57.48 0.000000041 ***
#> prev         1  15944   15944   57.45 0.000002552 ***
#> trt          5   4353     871    3.14       0.042 *  
#> Residuals   14   3885     278                        
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

The covariate is highly significant, and after adjusting for it the treatments differ significantly (\(F = 3.14\), \(p = 0.042\)). The error mean square has fallen from 1562 to 278: the covariate explained most of the variation that was previously treated as error. Precision is greatly improved:

EMS_anova  <- deviance(aov(yield ~ block + trt, data = apple)) / 15
EMS_ancova <- deviance(ancova) / df.residual(ancova)
EMS_anova / EMS_ancova     # approximate relative efficiency of ANCOVA
#> [1] 5.629

Because sequential sums of squares depend on the order of terms, it is good practice to check the result with Type II sums of squares from the car package, in which each term is adjusted for all others:

library(car)
Anova(lm(yield ~ block + prev + trt, data = apple), type = 2)
#> Anova Table (Type II tests)
#> 
#> Response: yield
#>           Sum Sq Df F value     Pr(>F)    
#> block      13258  3   15.92 0.00008638 ***
#> prev       19547  1   70.44 0.00000078 ***
#> trt         4353  5    3.14      0.042 *  
#> Residuals   3885 14                       
#> ---
#> Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

16.2.3 The regression coefficient 

coef(ancova)["prev"]
#> prev 
#> 28.4

Within blocks and treatments, each additional box of fruit in the earlier seasons is associated with 28.4 units of additional yield after treatment.

16.2.4 Testing homogeneity of slopes

The assumption of a common slope is tested by adding the treatment × covariate interaction:

anova(lm(yield ~ block + prev + trt, data = apple),
      lm(yield ~ block + prev * trt, data = apple))
#> Analysis of Variance Table
#> 
#> Model 1: yield ~ block + prev + trt
#> Model 2: yield ~ block + prev * trt
#>   Res.Df  RSS Df Sum of Sq    F Pr(>F)
#> 1     14 3885                         
#> 2      9 1776  5      2109 2.14   0.15

The interaction is not significant (\(p = 0.15\)), so a common slope is acceptable.

16.2.5 Adjusted treatment means

Adjusted means (also called least-squares means) are computed with emmeans, at the overall mean of the covariate:

library(emmeans)
adj <- emmeans(ancova, ~ trt)
adj
#>  trt emmean   SE df lower.CL upper.CL
#>  A      280 8.34 14      263      298
#>  B      267 8.33 14      249      284
#>  C      274 8.33 14      256      292
#>  D      281 8.43 14      263      299
#>  E      301 8.79 14      282      320
#>  S      251 8.98 14      232      271
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: block 
#> Confidence level used: 0.95
data.frame(
  unadjusted = tapply(apple$yield, apple$trt, mean),
  covariate_mean = tapply(apple$prev, apple$trt, mean),
  adjusted = summary(adj)$emmean
)
#>   unadjusted covariate_mean adjusted
#> A      284.5          8.450    280.5
#> B      267.8          8.350    266.6
#> C      275.2          8.350    274.1
#> D      270.2          7.925    281.1
#> E      277.2          7.475    300.9
#> S      279.5          9.300    251.3

The adjustment changes the picture considerably. Treatment S was given trees with the highest previous yields (mean 9.3), so its unadjusted mean (279.5) flattered it; after adjustment it is the lowest (251). Treatment E had the trees with the lowest previous yields, and its adjusted mean is the highest.

Compare each treatment with the standard practice S using Dunnett’s test:

contrast(adj, method = "trt.vs.ctrl", ref = "S")
#>  contrast estimate   SE df t.ratio p.value
#>  A - S        29.1 12.1 14   2.403  0.1148
#>  B - S        15.2 12.2 14   1.247  0.5993
#>  C - S        22.7 12.2 14   1.862  0.2773
#>  D - S        29.8 12.7 14   2.353  0.1253
#>  E - S        49.6 13.3 14   3.728  0.0096
#> 
#> Results are averaged over the levels of: block 
#> P value adjustment: dunnettx method for 5 tests

After adjusting for previous cropping, treatment E outyielded the standard practice significantly (\(p < 0.01\)); the other treatments did not differ significantly from S.

Covariance analysis for missing plots. Before computers were common, ANCOVA with dummy covariates was used to estimate missing values in designed experiments. Today, simply fit the model to the available data with lm() or aov() and obtain adjusted means with emmeans.

Exercises

  1. Using the apple data, compute the adjusted mean for treatment A by hand from the formula \(\bar y_{i(\text{adj})} = \bar y_{i.} - \hat\gamma(\bar x_{i.} - \bar x_{..})\) and check it against emmeans.
  2. Use Tukey’s method to compare all pairs of adjusted means. Which pairs differ significantly?
  3. The ChickWeight data contain the weight of chicks on four diets over time. Using the weight at day 21 as the response and the weight at day 0 as a covariate, test whether the diets differ.
