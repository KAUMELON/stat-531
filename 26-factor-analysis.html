28  Factor analysis

Learning outcomes

Explain the common factor model, communalities and uniqueness, check suitability of data, choose the number of factors, fit and rotate a maximum-likelihood factor model and interpret the factors.

Factor analysis (FA) explains the correlations among \(p\) observed variables in terms of a smaller number \(m\) of unobserved (latent) variables called common factors. Where PCA (Chapter 27) is a mathematical rearrangement of the total variance, factor analysis is a statistical model: it assumes that the observed variables are generated by the factors plus variable-specific errors.

28.1 The factor model

For standardised variables \(x_1, \ldots, x_p\),

\[x_i = \lambda_{i1} F_1 + \lambda_{i2} F_2 + \cdots + \lambda_{im} F_m + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, p\]

where

  • \(F_1, \ldots, F_m\) are the common factors (uncorrelated, with variance 1);
  • \(\lambda_{ij}\) is the loading of variable \(i\) on factor \(j\) (for uncorrelated factors, the correlation between \(x_i\) and \(F_j\));
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) is the specific factor of variable \(i\), with variance \(\psi_i\) (the uniqueness).

The variance of each standardised variable splits into

\[1 = \underbrace{\lambda_{i1}^2 + \cdots + \lambda_{im}^2}_{\text{communality } h_i^2} + \psi_i\]

The communality \(h_i^2\) is the part of the variance of \(x_i\) shared with the other variables through the common factors.

PCA Factor analysis
Aim summarise total variance explain correlations (common variance)
Model none; exact transformation \(x = \Lambda F + \varepsilon\)
Components / factors linear combinations of the variables latent variables generating the variables
Unique variance included in components separated as \(\psi_i\)

28.2 Steps in factor analysis

  1. Check that the data are suitable: the variables must be sufficiently correlated.
  2. Decide the number of factors.
  3. Estimate the loadings (here by maximum likelihood).
  4. Rotate the factors to make them easier to interpret.
  5. Name the factors from the variables that load highly on them, and compute factor scores if needed.

28.3 Example 28.1: traits of yacon

The yacon data in agricolae record 13 traits on yacon (Smallanthus sonchifolius) plants of eight entries at three localities in Peru: plant height, number of stalks, fresh weights of roots (wfr), foliage (wff) and crown (wfk), dry matter of roots (roots), foliage and crown (dry), and the contents of fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), glucose, fructose and sucrose (%) and degrees Brix. Can the correlations among these traits be explained by a few underlying factors?

library(agricolae)
data(yacon)
vars <- c("height", "stalks", "wfr", "wff", "wfk", "roots", "FOS",
          "glucose", "fructose", "sucrose", "brix", "foliage", "dry")
x <- yacon[, vars]
dim(x)
[1] 432  13

28.3.1 Step 1: suitability of the data

Bartlett’s test of sphericity tests whether the correlation matrix is an identity matrix (no correlations at all). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy compares correlations with partial correlations; values above 0.6 are considered adequate and above 0.8 good.

library(psych)
cortest.bartlett(cor(x), n = nrow(x))

28.3.2 Step 2: number of factors

Parallel analysis compares the eigenvalues of the data with those of random data of the same size; factors are retained while the observed eigenvalue exceeds the random one.

set.seed(531)
fa.parallel(x, fa = "fa", n.iter = 50, main = "Parallel analysis")
Parallel analysis suggests that the number of factors =  4  and the number of components =  NA

Parallel analysis suggests three or four factors. We first fit three factors for an easily interpreted solution.

28.3.3 Steps 3 and 4: estimation and rotation

factanal() (base R) fits the model by maximum likelihood. A varimax rotation keeps the factors uncorrelated while making each variable load highly on as few factors as possible.

fa3 <- factanal(x, factors = 3, rotation = "varimax", scores = "regression")
print(fa3$loadings, cutoff = 0.4, sort = TRUE)

28.3.4 Step 5: naming the factors

  • Factor 1: sugar composition: high positive loadings for glucose, number of stalks and fructose, and high negative loadings for FOS, Brix and dry matter. It contrasts plants rich in simple sugars with plants rich in FOS.
  • Factor 2: dry matter and soluble solids: dry root matter, Brix, sucrose and foliage dry matter.
  • Factor 3: fresh biomass: plant height and the fresh weights of roots, crown and foliage.
fa.diagram(fa3$loadings, main = "Three-factor solution (varimax)")

28.3.5 Goodness of fit

factanal() also tests whether \(m\) factors are sufficient:

c(chi_square = unname(fa3$STATISTIC), df = unname(fa3$dof), p_value = fa3$PVAL)
       chi_square                df p_value.objective 
        6.534e+02         4.200e+01        1.093e-110

The test rejects the three-factor model. With 432 observations the test is very sensitive, and it rejects even the four-factor model; in practice the number of factors is chosen for interpretability and from parallel analysis rather than from this test alone.

28.3.6 Factor scores

Factor scores estimate the value of each factor for each plant and can be used in further analysis, for example to compare localities:

scores <- as.data.frame(fa3$scores)
loc_scores <- aggregate(scores, by = list(locality = yacon$locality), FUN = mean)
loc_scores[, -1] <- round(loc_scores[, -1], 2)
loc_scores
  locality Factor1 Factor2 Factor3
1      CAJ   -0.16    0.04   -0.86
2      LIM    0.96   -0.41    0.69
3      OXA   -0.80    0.37    0.17

Plants at Lima score high on Factor 1 (more simple sugars, less FOS) and on fresh biomass (Factor 3); plants at Oxapampa are the richest in FOS; plants at Cajamarca have the lowest fresh biomass.

28.3.7 Oblique rotation

The pattern of loadings is very similar to the varimax solution. With an oblique rotation the loadings are regression weights rather than correlations, so a value slightly above 1 (as for roots) can occur. The sugar-composition and dry-matter factors are fairly strongly correlated (about −0.63), which suggests that an oblique solution describes these traits more faithfully than an orthogonal one.

28.4 Live R laboratory

Factor analysis of ability tests

Edit the code, then select Run R. The first run downloads R into your browser and may take a little time. Copy code lets you use the example in RStudio.

# Covariances of six ability tests on 112 people (built-in data set)
fa2 <- factanal(factors = 2, covmat = ability.cov, rotation = "varimax")
print(fa2$loadings, cutoff = 0.3)
round(1 - fa2$uniquenesses, 2)          # communalities
fa2$PVAL                                # is two factors enough?

Investigate. Name the two factors from the tests that load on them. Then fit one factor (factors = 1) and compare the goodness-of-fit p-value.

Historical Insights

Charles Spearman introduced factor analysis in 1904 to explain the correlations among school examination marks by a single general factor of intelligence.

Numerical and conceptual questions

Answers are given at the end of the chapter.

  1. A standardised variable loads 0.8 and 0.3 on two uncorrelated factors. Find its communality and uniqueness.
  2. Three factors have SS loadings 3.169, 3.141 and 3.088 for 13 variables. What proportion of variance do they explain together?

Important formulae

Common factor model:

\[ x_i = \lambda_{i1}F_1 + \cdots + \lambda_{im}F_m + \varepsilon_i \tag{28.1}\]

Communality and uniqueness of a standardised variable:

\[ h_i^2 = \sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_{ij}^2, \qquad \psi_i = 1 - h_i^2 \tag{28.2}\]

Quick revision

  • PCA → total variance; FA → common variance through latent factors.
  • Check suitability: Bartlett significant, KMO > 0.6.
  • Number of factors → parallel analysis and interpretability.
  • Varimax → uncorrelated factors; promax → correlated factors.
  • Name factors from the variables with high loadings.

1. common 2. communality 3. uniqueness 4. KMO 5. random 6. orthogonal 7. oblique 8. likelihood

Solutions to numerical and conceptual questions

  2. \((3.169 + 3.141 + 3.088)/13 = 72.3\%\).
