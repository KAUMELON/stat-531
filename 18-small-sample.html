20  Tests based on t, F and chi-square

Learning outcomes

Carry out one-sample, two-sample (pooled and Welch) and paired t tests, the F test for variances, Bartlett’s test, the chi-square test for a variance, goodness of fit to genetic ratios and tests of independence in contingency tables.

When samples are small, the normal approximation of Chapter 19 is no longer adequate if the population variance has to be estimated. Exact tests are then based on the t, F and \(\chi^2\) distributions, assuming that the observations come from normal populations. This chapter covers:

  • t tests for one mean, two independent means and paired observations
  • F test for two variances, and tests for several variances
  • \(\chi^2\) test for a single variance
  • \(\chi^2\) tests of goodness of fit and of independence in contingency tables
  • non-parametric alternatives

20.1 The t test for a single mean

To test \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) from a sample of size \(n\) from a normal population,

\[t = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}\]

follows Student’s t distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom under \(H_0\).

Example 20.1 The PlantGrowth data (Section 3.7) give the dried weight (g) of plants grown under a control and two treatments, 10 plants each. Is the mean weight of control plants equal to 5.5 g?

Solution

ctrl <- PlantGrowth$weight[PlantGrowth$group == "ctrl"]
ctrl
 [1] 4.17 5.58 5.18 6.11 4.50 4.61 5.17 4.53 5.33 5.14
t.test(ctrl, mu = 5.5)

    One Sample t-test

data:  ctrl
t = -2.5, df = 9, p-value = 0.03
alternative hypothesis: true mean is not equal to 5.5
95 percent confidence interval:
 4.615 5.449
sample estimates:
mean of x 
    5.032

\(t = -2.54\) with 9 df and \(p = 0.032 < 0.05\), so we reject \(H_0\): the mean weight of control plants (5.03 g) is significantly less than 5.5 g. The output also gives the 95% confidence interval for the mean, 4.61 to 5.45 g, which does not contain 5.5.

Use alternative = "less" or "greater" for a one-sided test, and conf.level = 0.99 for a 99% interval.

20.2 The t test for two independent means

To compare the means of two independent samples, \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\).

Equal variances (Student’s t test). If the two populations have the same variance,

\[t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{s_p \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{n_1} + \dfrac{1}{n_2}}}, \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

with \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) df, where \(s_p^2\) is the pooled variance.

Unequal variances (Welch’s t test). Otherwise,

\[t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

with degrees of freedom given by Satterthwaite’s approximation.

Since the choice depends on whether the variances are equal, first test that with the F test.

20.2.1 The F test for two variances

To test \(H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\),

\[F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}\]

follows the F distribution with \((n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)\) df under \(H_0\). (When doing the test by hand, put the larger variance in the numerator and compare with the upper critical value.)

Example 20.2 Compare the control with treatment 2 in the PlantGrowth data.

Solution

pg <- droplevels(subset(PlantGrowth, group %in% c("ctrl", "trt2")))
tapply(pg$weight, pg$group, var)
  ctrl   trt2 
0.3400 0.1959 
var.test(weight ~ group, data = pg)

    F test to compare two variances

data:  weight by group
F = 1.7, num df = 9, denom df = 9, p-value = 0.4
alternative hypothesis: true ratio of variances is not equal to 1
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.4312 6.9884
sample estimates:
ratio of variances 
             1.736

\(F = 1.74\) with (9, 9) df, \(p = 0.42\), so the variances do not differ significantly and the pooled t test is appropriate:

t.test(weight ~ group, data = pg, var.equal = TRUE)

    Two Sample t-test

data:  weight by group
t = -2.1, df = 18, p-value = 0.05
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group ctrl and group trt2 is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.980338 -0.007662
sample estimates:
mean in group ctrl mean in group trt2 
             5.032              5.526

\(t = -2.13\) with 18 df and \(p = 0.047 < 0.05\): plants under treatment 2 (mean 5.53 g) are significantly heavier than control plants (5.03 g).

Without var.equal = TRUE, R performs Welch’s test, which is the default and is safe to use whether or not the variances are equal:

t.test(weight ~ group, data = pg)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  weight by group
t = -2.1, df = 17, p-value = 0.05
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group ctrl and group trt2 is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.982872 -0.005128
sample estimates:
mean in group ctrl mean in group trt2 
             5.032              5.526

Here both tests lead to the same conclusion.

Note

In the formula weight ~ group, the response is on the left of ~ and the grouping factor on the right. You can also give the two samples as separate vectors: t.test(x, y).

20.3 The paired t test

When observations come in pairs (the same plot before and after treatment, two treatments applied to the two halves of a leaf, two plants in the same pot), the two samples are not independent. The analysis is then carried out on the differences \(d_i = x_i - y_i\):

\[t = \frac{\bar d}{s_d / \sqrt{n}}\]

with \(n - 1\) df, where \(\bar d\) and \(s_d\) are the mean and SD of the differences.

Example 20.3 Charles Darwin compared the heights of cross-fertilised and self-fertilised maize (Zea mays) plants. Fifteen pairs of plants, one of each type, were grown in the same pots, so each pair shares the same conditions. The data, used by Fisher in The Design of Experiments (Fisher 1935), are in the HistData package (heights in inches):

Solution

library(HistData)
ZeaMays[, c("pair", "pot", "cross", "self", "diff")]
   pair pot cross  self   diff
1     1   1 23.50 17.38  6.125
2     2   1 12.00 20.38 -8.375
3     3   1 21.00 20.00  1.000
4     4   2 22.00 20.00  2.000
5     5   2 19.12 18.38  0.750
6     6   2 21.50 18.62  2.875
7     7   3 22.12 18.62  3.500
8     8   3 20.38 15.25  5.125
9     9   3 18.25 16.50  1.750
10   10   3 21.62 18.00  3.625
11   11   3 23.25 16.25  7.000
12   12   4 21.00 18.00  3.000
13   13   4 22.12 12.75  9.375
14   14   4 23.00 15.50  7.500
15   15   4 12.00 18.00 -6.000
t.test(ZeaMays$cross, ZeaMays$self, paired = TRUE)

    Paired t-test

data:  ZeaMays$cross and ZeaMays$self
t = 2.1, df = 14, p-value = 0.05
alternative hypothesis: true mean difference is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.003899 5.229434
sample estimates:
mean difference 
          2.617

The mean difference is 2.62 inches, \(t = 2.15\) with 14 df, \(p = 0.0497\): cross-fertilised plants are significantly taller than self-fertilised plants at the 5% level (but not at 1%).

Warning

Analysing paired data with an independent two-sample test ignores the pairing and usually loses power, because the variation between pairs is then included in the error. Always use the test that matches how the data were collected.

20.4 Testing several variances

Bartlett’s test compares the variances of \(k \ge 2\) groups, assuming normality. Levene’s test (in the car package) is less sensitive to non-normality. Both are used to check the homogeneity-of-variance assumption of ANOVA (Chapter 21).

bartlett.test(weight ~ group, data = PlantGrowth)

    Bartlett test of homogeneity of variances

data:  weight by group
Bartlett's K-squared = 2.9, df = 2, p-value = 0.2
library(car)
Loading required package: carData
leveneTest(weight ~ group, data = PlantGrowth)
Levene's Test for Homogeneity of Variance (center = median)
      Df F value Pr(>F)
group  2    1.12   0.34
      27

Neither test is significant (\(p > 0.05\)), so the three groups have similar variances.

20.5 Chi-square test for a single variance

To test \(H_0: \sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2\) for a normal population,

\[\chi^2 = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}\]

with \(n - 1\) df. R has no built-in function, but the calculation is short.

Example 20.4 Is the variance of control plant weights equal to 0.25 g2?

Solution

n <- length(ctrl)
s2 <- var(ctrl)
sigma0_sq <- 0.25
chi <- (n - 1) * s2 / sigma0_sq
p_value <- 2 * min(pchisq(chi, n - 1), pchisq(chi, n - 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
c(variance = s2, chi_square = chi, df = n - 1, p_value = p_value)
  variance chi_square         df    p_value 
    0.3400    12.2398     9.0000     0.4002

\(p = 0.40\), so the data are consistent with \(\sigma^2 = 0.25\).

20.6 Chi-square test of goodness of fit

The test of goodness of fit (Chapter 18) also tests whether observed frequencies agree with a ratio expected from theory, as in Mendelian genetics:

\[\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}, \qquad \text{df} = k - 1\]

Example 20.5 In his dihybrid cross of peas, Mendel observed 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green seeds. Do these agree with the expected 9:3:3:1 ratio?

Solution

observed <- c(round_yellow = 315, round_green = 108,
              wrinkled_yellow = 101, wrinkled_green = 32)
res <- chisq.test(observed, p = c(9, 3, 3, 1) / 16)
res

    Chi-squared test for given probabilities

data:  observed
X-squared = 0.47, df = 3, p-value = 0.9
res$expected
   round_yellow     round_green wrinkled_yellow  wrinkled_green 
         312.75          104.25          104.25           34.75

\(\chi^2 = 0.47\) with 3 df, \(p = 0.93\): the data agree very well with the 9:3:3:1 ratio.

20.7 Chi-square test of independence

A contingency table classifies individuals by two attributes. To test whether the attributes are independent, the expected frequency of each cell is

\[E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{row } i \text{ total}) \times (\text{column } j \text{ total})}{\text{grand total}}\]

and \(\chi^2 = \sum (O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}\) has \((r-1)(c-1)\) df for an \(r \times c\) table.

Example 20.6 In an F2 population from a cross of white sweet maize with yellow starchy maize, the kernels on one ear were classified by colour and texture [Pearl, 1911; pearl.kernels in agridat]. If the two genes segregate independently, colour and texture should be independent.

Solution

library(agridat)
d <- subset(pearl.kernels, ear == "Ear08" & obs == "Obs01")
tab <- matrix(c(d$ys, d$yt, d$ws, d$wt), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE,
              dimnames = list(Colour = c("Yellow", "White"),
                              Texture = c("Starchy", "Sweet")))
tab
        Texture
Colour   Starchy Sweet
  Yellow     352   102
  White       52    26
chisq.test(tab)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction

data:  tab
X-squared = 3.7, df = 1, p-value = 0.05
chisq.test(tab)$expected
        Texture
Colour   Starchy  Sweet
  Yellow  344.77 109.23
  White    59.23  18.77

For a 2 × 2 table, chisq.test() applies Yates’ correction for continuity by default. Here \(\chi^2 = 3.73\) with 1 df and \(p = 0.054\), which is just above 0.05. Without the correction:

chisq.test(tab, correct = FALSE)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  tab
X-squared = 4.3, df = 1, p-value = 0.04

the p-value is 0.038. When the conclusion depends on the correction, or when expected frequencies are small (below 5), use Fisher’s exact test:

fisher.test(tab)

    Fisher's Exact Test for Count Data

data:  tab
p-value = 0.04
alternative hypothesis: true odds ratio is not equal to 1
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.9812 2.9746
sample estimates:
odds ratio 
     1.723

Fisher’s exact test gives \(p = 0.045\), weak evidence of an association between colour and texture in this ear. The proportion of sweet kernels is higher among white kernels (33%) than among yellow kernels (22%).

Note

For an \(r \times c\) table, give chisq.test() the table of counts. If you have raw data with one row per individual, make the table first with table(data$A, data$B) or xtabs(~ A + B, data).

20.8 Non-parametric alternatives

When the data are clearly non-normal and the sample is small, rank-based tests make no assumption about the distribution:

Parametric test Non-parametric alternative R function
One-sample or paired t Wilcoxon signed-rank wilcox.test(x, mu = ), wilcox.test(x, y, paired = TRUE)
Two-sample t Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) wilcox.test(y ~ group)
One-way ANOVA Kruskal–Wallis kruskal.test(y ~ group)
RCB ANOVA Friedman friedman.test(y ~ trt | block) 
wilcox.test(ZeaMays$cross, ZeaMays$self, paired = TRUE, exact = FALSE)

    Wilcoxon signed rank test with continuity correction

data:  ZeaMays$cross and ZeaMays$self
V = 96, p-value = 0.04
alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

The signed-rank test also finds a significant difference (\(p = 0.044\)) for Darwin’s maize data.

Summary: which test?

Question Test R function
Is a mean equal to a given value? one-sample t t.test(x, mu = )
Do two independent means differ? two-sample t (pooled or Welch) t.test(y ~ g, var.equal = )
Do paired observations differ? paired t t.test(x, y, paired = TRUE)
Are two variances equal? F test var.test()
Are several variances equal? Bartlett, Levene bartlett.test(), car::leveneTest()
Do frequencies follow a ratio? \(\chi^2\) goodness of fit chisq.test(x, p = )
Are two attributes associated? \(\chi^2\) independence, Fisher exact chisq.test(tab), fisher.test(tab)

20.9 Live R laboratory

Darwin’s maize: paired versus unpaired

Edit the code, then select Run R. The first run downloads R into your browser and may take a little time. Copy code lets you use the example in RStudio.

cross <- c(23.5, 12, 21, 22, 19.125, 21.5, 22.125, 20.375, 18.25, 21.625,
           23.25, 21, 22.125, 23, 12)
self  <- c(17.375, 20.375, 20, 20, 18.375, 18.625, 18.625, 15.25, 16.5,
           18, 16.25, 18, 12.75, 15.5, 18)
t.test(cross, self, paired = TRUE)        # correct: plants paired in pots
t.test(cross, self, var.equal = TRUE)     # ignores the pairing

# Mendel's dihybrid ratio 9:3:3:1
chisq.test(c(315, 108, 101, 32), p = c(9, 3, 3, 1) / 16)

Investigate. Compare the two t tests. Why does the paired test use 14 degrees of freedom, and why do the two p-values differ?

Historical Insights

Charles Darwin grew his crossed and self-fertilised maize plants in pairs and published the heights in 1876; R. A. Fisher reanalysed them in The Design of Experiments (1935). George W. Snedecor named the F distribution in Fisher’s honour in 1934.

Important formulae

Pooled variance and two-sample t:

\[ s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}, \qquad t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} \tag{20.1}\]

F test for two variances:

\[ F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} \tag{20.2}\]

Chi-square test for a variance:

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2} \tag{20.3}\]

Expected frequency in a contingency table:

\[ E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \times C_j}{N} \tag{20.4}\]

Quick revision

  • One mean → one-sample t; two independent means → pooled or Welch t; pairs → paired t.
  • Two variances → F; several variances → Bartlett or Levene.
  • Ratios → chi-square goodness of fit; association → chi-square or Fisher’s test.
  • Small, non-normal samples → rank tests.
  • Match the test to how the data were collected.
